# The grid decade: a study of Germany's energy transition and E.ON

*An independent analysis by Jeel Swami · August 2026 · [Interactive version](https://jeelswami.github.io/energiewende-decoded/)*

## Summary

Five findings carry this study.

**First, the Energiewende has a success problem.** Renewable sources covered a record 55.1 percent of German electricity demand in 2025. The same year produced a record 573 hours of negative day-ahead prices, solar capture prices below 20 €/MWh, and 3.1 billion euros of congestion management costs. Generation is no longer the constraint. The grid, and the flexibility of the system around it, is.

**Second, the bottleneck is measurable in the connection queue.** By late 2025, grid connection requests for large battery storage exceeded 500 GW nationwide; a BDEW survey counted more than 720 GW. The national peak load is roughly 80 GW, and only about 2.5 GW of large storage was actually operating. E.ON alone reported storage requests of roughly 330 GW. Speculative requests hold back real, financeable load: data centres, industry, and the reinforcement work of the heat pump era.

**Third, capital decides everything.** Germany's electricity grids need on the order of 360 billion euros by 2045 on BNetzA-based figures, and industry estimates reach 651 billion. Distribution operators alone need about 110 billion by 2033. A study by KfW Research and PwC puts the sector's financing gap at 346 billion euros; utilities can self-fund roughly a quarter of what the transition asks of them. Whoever solves the equity question builds the grid.

**Fourth, E.ON is the clearest expression of this decade.** After the asset swap with RWE, E.ON is Europe's largest distribution operator and energy retailer. In 2025 it reported adjusted EBITDA of 9.8 billion euros, of which 7.7 billion came from networks, invested a record 8.5 billion, and announced a 48 billion euro programme for 2026 to 2030 with an EBITDA ambition of about 13 billion by 2030. Its constraints, regulatory returns under the new NEST framework, the queue, financing headroom, and retail commoditisation, are the sector's constraints in miniature.

**Fifth, four practical questions follow**, and they are the substance of section 4: how to order the queue, how to finance the programme, how to make household flexibility a product, and where AI genuinely defers capital spending.

## 1 · When success creates the problem

Germany hit its renewable build-out targets faster than its infrastructure could absorb them. Installed solar capacity has more than doubled since 2020, reaching about 118 GW at the end of 2025, and wind and solar took first and second place in public net generation that year. Three friction indicators tell the other half of the story.

| Indicator | Latest value | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Hours with negative day-ahead prices | 573 in 2025, a record; 457 in 2024 (FfE) | Midday solar routinely exceeds demand, export and storage capacity together |
| Solar capture price | below 20 €/MWh in May 2025, 37 percent lower than a year before (S&P Global) | Cannibalisation erodes the business case of unsubsidised solar |
| Congestion management cost | 3.1 billion euros in 2025; the peak was 4.2 billion in 2022 (BNetzA) | The grid cannot move power from where it is made to where it is used |

Policy has begun to react. The Solarspitzengesetz removed support payments during negative price hours for new solar plants. Paragraph 14a of the EnWG, in force since 2024, lets distribution operators reduce the draw of controllable devices to a guaranteed minimum of 4.2 kW in exchange for lower network charges. Dynamic retail tariffs became mandatory for all suppliers in January 2025. In December 2025 the Bundesnetzagentur's NEST determinations rebuilt the return framework for grid investment. And the government that took office in 2025 recalibrated: its energy transition monitoring report shifted weight toward demand realism, hydrogen targets are being scaled back (181 MW of electrolysis stands against the old 10 GW goal for 2030), and a 6.5 billion euro federal subsidy lowered average transmission charges by about 57 percent for 2026.

Each of these mechanisms, queue reform, NEST parameters, §14a flexibility, tariff design, is a live regulatory construction site where analytical capacity is scarce and the stakes are measured in billions. That scarcity is, frankly, the opportunity for people who can do the analysis.

## 2 · The grid bottleneck, quantified

**The investment need.** BNetzA-based figures put electricity grid investment at about 360 billion euros to 2045, roughly 160 billion for transmission and 200 billion for distribution, plus about 160 billion for offshore connections. The draft network development plan for 2037 and 2045 estimates 360 to 390 billion, with up to 80 billion of savings available through better planning. An industry study arrives at 651 billion all-in. For distribution alone, a BDEW and ZVEI analysis by the University of Wuppertal estimates 323 billion euros, including about 34,500 km of new high-voltage, 262,000 km of medium-voltage and 526,000 km of low-voltage lines.

**The queue.** Storage connection requests passed 500 GW nationally in September 2025; the BDEW survey of four transmission and 17 large distribution operators counted more than 720 GW. Only about 2.5 GW of large-scale battery storage was in operation at the end of the year, which is the clearest sign that the queue consists mostly of options rather than projects. Behind it waits bankable demand: at E.ON's distribution subsidiary Mitnetz Strom, 90 data centre requests total 16.8 GW, out of roughly 3,100 requests in that network area. The emerging remedy, a maturity test (Reifegradverfahren) that prioritises projects with land, permits and financing, may be the highest-leverage administrative reform available in the sector.

**The financing gap.** KfW Research and PwC estimate that the regional energy and heat transition needs 535 billion euros by 2045, of which utilities can self-fund about a quarter. The remaining 346 billion would have to come from new equity (about 47 billion) and debt (about 299 billion) at typical capital structures. EnBW's capital increase of about 3.1 billion euros shows the constraint is already binding. The policy debate, from BDEW's proposal of an Energiewende fund to the Amortisationskonto idea of stretching grid cost recovery across decades and the 2026 transmission fee subsidy, is at bottom one question: who carries the working capital of the transition, taxpayers, ratepayers or investors.

## 3 · E.ON, in detail

**How it came to be what it is.** Three corporate events shaped E.ON: the merger of VEBA and VIAG in 2000 created it, the spin-off of Uniper in 2016 removed conventional generation, and the innogy transaction with RWE, worth about 43 billion euros and completed in 2020, finished the de-integration. RWE took generation and renewables, plus a 16.7 percent stake in E.ON; E.ON took innogy's networks and retail. The result is a regulated asset base compounder whose growth is bounded not by demand but by regulatory returns, financing capacity and execution speed.

**Scale.** About 76,800 employees. Roughly 46.8 million retail customers across Europe, 13.6 million of them in Germany. Six major German distribution operators (Westnetz, Avacon, Bayernwerk, E.DIS, HanseWerk, Mitnetz) running about 700,000 km of grid, more than a third of the German distribution network, with about 110 GW of renewable capacity connected, roughly 70 percent of German onshore wind and half of German solar. S&P rates the company BBB+ with a stable outlook (affirmed May 2026); the market capitalisation stood near 46 to 50 billion euros in mid-2026 after a share price gain of about 68 percent in 2025.

**The 2025 numbers.** Adjusted EBITDA of 9.8 billion euros, up 9 percent: 7.7 billion from Energy Networks (up 12 percent), 1.7 billion from Energy Retail (down 6 percent), about 0.6 billion from Energy Infrastructure Solutions. Adjusted net income of 3.0 billion. Record investment of 8.5 billion, 7.0 billion of it in networks. The company plans 48 billion euros of investment from 2026 to 2030, targets more than 11.3 billion of EBITDA by 2028 and about 13 billion by 2030, and grows its dividend by up to 5 percent a year (0.57 euros per share for 2025).

**Evidence of execution.** E.ON's answer to the queue is already digital: a grid digital twin, built with envelio, covers the full 700,000 km and 55 million components and automates the assessment of more than 410,000 connection requests a year. Its chief executive Leonhard Birnbaum expects the company will need to make "one connection every seven seconds of a working day" by 2030 (Eurelectric interview). In metering, E.ON was the first German utility past one million smart meters, a rollout rate near 30 percent against the statutory 20 percent, and nearly half of all smart meters in Germany. In new demand, the CyrusOne partnership of June 2025 will add 61 MW of on-site generation to Frankfurt's FRA7 data centre campus by 2029, a template for connecting large loads despite a constrained grid.

**What could stop it.** Four things. Regulatory returns, if NEST prices equity too thinly against rising rates. The queue, which consumes engineering capacity and political goodwill. Financing, because 48 billion euros over five years lands in a sector with a measured equity constraint. And retail margin pressure, because dynamic tariffs and price transparency commoditise supply, so the cost of serving a customer becomes the differentiator. E.ON's roughly 70 percent automation rate in conversational customer service shows the transformation is under way, and how far it still has to go.

## 4 · Four questions, four answers

### 1 · How should the queue be ordered?

By maturity, not arrival time. Assess requests against published criteria (secured land, permits, financing), charge refundable reservation fees so speculation carries a price, publish hosting capacity maps from the digital twin so developers can find feasible nodes themselves, and offer curtailable connection agreements as a fast lane for storage and data centres.
*Indicators: median time to a connection offer; share of the queue with demonstrated maturity; GW of flexible connection contracts signed.*

### 2 · How does the programme get financed?

By treating 48 billion euros as a portfolio rather than a construction plan: recycle capital through minority stakes in mature grid assets, which infrastructure funds price richly; support instruments that stretch cost recovery across generations, in the spirit of the Amortisationskonto; and argue the NEST equity return case with quantified evidence about the cost of under-remunerated capital.
*Indicators: FFO to net debt within the rating corridor; realised asset base growth against plan; the spread of allowed returns over the cost of capital.*

### 3 · What does retail become?

A flexibility platform. With 573 negative price hours in 2025, §14a control rights, time variable network charges and mandatory dynamic tariffs, household flexibility is tradable for the first time. A bundle of dynamic tariff, wallbox, heat pump and home battery can shift load into cheap hours and sell aggregated flexibility into congestion management.
*Indicators: MW of §14a-controlled load under management; smart meter penetration against the legal trajectory of 20 percent in 2025 toward 95 percent; churn in flexibility bundles against commodity contracts.*

### 4 · Where does AI actually pay?

Where it defers capital spending. Probabilistic load flow on the digital twin turns planning cycles of months into days and postpones physical reinforcement; the McKinsey demand-realism argument suggests right-sizing could cut system investment by tens of percent. Field force assistance matters in a country with more than 18,000 unfilled electrician positions. And existing customer service automation can extend into tariff advice. All of it inside the NIS2 and KRITIS rules, since cybersecurity became a board duty in Germany in December 2025.
*Indicators: planning cycle time; investment deferred per euro of digital spending; cost to serve per customer; automation rate with a customer satisfaction floor.*
